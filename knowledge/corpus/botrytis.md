## Symptoms

A fuzzy, grey-brown mould grows on dead, damaged, or senescent plant tissue,
especially spent flowers, fallen leaves, and wounded stems, in cool, humid
conditions. The mould can spread from dead tissue onto adjoining healthy tissue,
causing soft, brown, collapsing patches.

## Where on the plant symptoms appear

Wherever dead or damaged tissue sits in still, humid air: spent flowers, leaf
litter caught in the plant, and stem wounds. It spreads outward from there onto
healthy tissue it touches.

## Look-alikes and how to tell them apart

Powdery mildew is a dry, white, dusty coating on living leaf surfaces rather than
a fuzzy grey growth confined to dead or damaged tissue. Root rot rots roots and
the stem base from waterlogged soil below ground, not aerial fuzzy mould on
flowers or upper stems. Sooty mould is a black surface film tied to a pest's
honeydew, not a fuzzy grey growth on dying plant material.

## Confirming test the user can perform

Look for grey, fuzzy mould growth on dead or damaged plant parts, especially
spent flowers or wounded stems, in a cool, humid spot. Gently disturb it: fine
grey spores puff visibly into the air.

## Treatment, least-invasive first

Improve airflow and reduce humidity around the plant. Remove spent flowers,
fallen leaves, and any mouldy tissue promptly, cutting back into clean tissue
with sterilised scissors. Avoid wetting leaves and flowers when watering. Space
the plant away from others to reduce humidity buildup. Only if mould keeps
returning despite these changes, apply a fungicide labelled for botrytis,
following the product label exactly.

## Expected time to visible improvement

No new mould growth within a week of improving airflow and removing affected
tissue.

## Prognosis and when to give up

Good if caught before it reaches the main stem. Mould that has girdled a main
stem or growing point is a poor sign and that section is unlikely to recover.
