## Symptoms

Wilting, leaf drop, or a generally droopy appearance in the days to weeks after
repotting, moving the plant, or bringing it home from a shop. Growth stalls. Some
leaves may yellow and fall even though watering has not changed.

## Where on the plant symptoms appear

Scattered across the plant rather than following a position pattern — older and
newer leaves can drop together. Sometimes the most recent, still-soft growth wilts
first because it has the least established root connection.

## Look-alikes and how to tell them apart

Root rot causes similar wilting but the soil is wet and roots are mushy; transplant
shock occurs with normal watering and firm roots. Underwatering recovers within
hours of watering, whereas transplant shock does not improve with watering alone
and instead fades gradually over days to weeks. Cold draught causes sudden damage
tied to a specific exposure, not a gradual settling-in period after a move.

## Confirming test the user can perform

Check the timeline: did symptoms start within one to two weeks of repotting,
moving the plant to a new spot, or a change in transport or environment? If yes,
and the roots (checked by gently tipping the plant from its pot) are firm and
pale, this is shock rather than rot or a nutrient problem.

## Treatment, least-invasive first

Leave the plant alone as much as possible: same spot, consistent light, no
fertiliser until new growth resumes. Keep watering steady rather than compensating
with extra water, and raise humidity slightly if the air is very dry. Avoid
repotting again or moving the plant further while it settles. Trim off any leaves
that are fully collapsed to reduce water demand.

## Expected time to visible improvement

Most plants stabilise within one to three weeks. New growth is the clearest sign
of recovery.

## Prognosis and when to give up

Excellent for most houseplants provided the root system was intact at transplant
and conditions afterward stay stable. Give it a full growing season before judging
a repot a failure.
