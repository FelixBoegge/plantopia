## Symptoms

Silvery or bronze streaking and fine stippling appear on leaves, often
accompanied by tiny black flecks of excrement (frass). New growth can look
distorted or streaked, and flowers may show streaks or fail to open properly.
Leaves eventually turn papery and dull.

## Where on the plant symptoms appear

New growth and flowers are affected most, since thrips prefer soft, actively
growing tissue. Streaking spreads along the leaf as feeding continues.

## Look-alikes and how to tell them apart

Spider mites produce fine, even stippling and webbing but not the silvery
streaking or black frass specks thrips leave behind. Aphids cluster visibly on
new growth and leave sticky honeydew rather than streaked, papery tissue.
Chemical damage causes irregular patchy marks tied to a specific exposure event,
not the streaking that spreads with continued feeding.

## Confirming test the user can perform

Hold a sheet of white paper under a leaf or flower and tap firmly. Thrips fall as
tiny, elongated, pale-yellow to dark specks that move quickly across the paper.
Check flowers and new leaves closely for silvery streaks and black frass dots.

## Treatment, least-invasive first

Isolate the plant immediately, since thrips move readily to nearby plants.
Remove and discard the most heavily streaked leaves and flowers. Rinse the plant
thoroughly, focusing on new growth and flower buds, and repeat every few days for
two weeks. Blue or yellow sticky traps help monitor and reduce adult numbers.
Introduce predatory mites or lacewings if the infestation persists, and only turn
to an insecticidal soap as a last resort, following the product label exactly.

## Expected time to visible improvement

No new streaking within two weeks. Existing streaked or distorted growth does
not repair itself.

## Prognosis and when to give up

Good with sustained treatment. Thrips have a fast life cycle, so stopping
treatment early after symptoms fade commonly leads to a rebound.
