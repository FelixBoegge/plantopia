## Symptoms

Small white, cottony masses appear in leaf axils, along stems, and on leaf
undersides. A sticky honeydew coats nearby leaves, sometimes with a black sooty
mould growing on it. Growth slows and leaves can yellow or drop where colonies
are dense.

## Where on the plant symptoms appear

Leaf joints (axils), the undersides of leaves along the midrib, and crevices
where stems meet, since mealybugs favour sheltered spots.

## Look-alikes and how to tell them apart

Aphids also cluster and leave honeydew but appear as visible soft-bodied insects
of various colours, not white cottony fluff. Scale insects produce a similar
sticky residue but sit as flat, hard, immobile bumps rather than fluffy white
masses. Powdery mildew leaves a white coating too, but it dusts the leaf surface
evenly rather than forming discrete cottony clumps in leaf joints.

## Confirming test the user can perform

Look closely in leaf axils and along the stem for small white, cottony tufts. Use
a hand lens if available: the fluff surrounds a soft, oval insect body
underneath, distinguishing it from a fungal coating.

## Treatment, least-invasive first

Isolate the plant immediately. Dab visible masses with a cotton swab dipped in
rubbing alcohol, which dissolves their waxy coating on contact. Rinse the plant
thoroughly afterward. Repeat every few days for two to three weeks to catch
newly hatched insects. Introduce mealybug predators such as ladybird species
bred for this pest if the infestation is widespread. Only as a last resort, apply
an insecticidal soap or horticultural oil, following the product label exactly.

## Expected time to visible improvement

No new cottony masses within two to three weeks. Existing honeydew residue can be
wiped away once the colony is gone.

## Prognosis and when to give up

Good with persistence. Mealybugs hide in crevices and hatch over an extended
period, so treatment needs to continue well past when symptoms first look better.
